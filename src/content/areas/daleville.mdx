## Two Dalevilles, radon-wise

Daleville's housing does not sort into one category, and the practical consequence is that generic radon advice fits about half the town.

**The older half.** Original town-center homes with block foundations, partial basements, and crawl spaces, many with additions from various decades. These have the classic older-construction entry paths: hollow block cores open to the soil, settled slabs with joint separation, unsealed crawl space floors, and sump pits with no lid. They are also the homes most likely to have a finished basement in daily use, which puts people in the highest-concentration space in the house.

**The newer half.** Slab-on-grade construction, tighter envelopes, better windows. The entry paths are fewer but they still exist, at the perimeter joint, at control joints, and at every plumbing and conduit penetration. And the tighter envelope means less accidental dilution of what does come in. Some of these homes may have a passive radon rough-in from the builder, a capped pipe running from below the slab up through the roof. If yours does, activation is much cheaper than a retrofit, so it is worth going up in the attic to look.

The reason this matters when you call: knowing which one you have lets us tell you a great deal before anyone drives anywhere.

## Sitting at the county line

Daleville is close enough to the Madison County line that people occasionally ask whether the area is somehow better or worse than Muncie for radon. The honest answer is that the question does not resolve the way it seems like it should.

Delaware County is EPA Zone 1. Madison County, immediately west, is also Zone 1. Both designations mean the same thing: the predicted average indoor screening level for the county exceeds the 4.0 pCi/L action level.

More importantly, EPA zones are county-level administrative categories built from geology, soil permeability, and available indoor measurements. They were never intended to predict a specific address. Within any Zone 1 county there are homes at 0.7 and homes at 20, sometimes on the same block, because foundation construction and how a particular house moves air matter enormously at the property level.

Which is the whole argument for testing rather than reasoning from a map.

## The commuter household pattern

Daleville's position near I-69 means a lot of households here commute to Anderson or Indianapolis, and that daily pattern interacts with radon in a way worth understanding.

An empty house is a closed house. Doors stay shut, windows stay shut, and there is no cooking, laundry, or door traffic driving incidental air exchange. Radon entering from the soil accumulates through those hours rather than being diluted.

The result is that concentrations tend to be highest in the evening and overnight, which is when the house is occupied and when people are asleep in bedrooms for the longest continuous stretch of the day. Exposure is not evenly distributed across the clock, and the peak lines up with the hours you are home.

This also explains the closed-house protocol for testing. A 48-hour test requires the house sealed for 12 hours beforehand and throughout, precisely so the measurement reflects the conditions the house actually runs under. A test conducted with windows open during a nice week describes a house nobody lives in. Details on the [radon testing page](/services/radon-testing/).

## Buying or selling in Daleville

Radon comes up in Delaware County transactions constantly, and Daleville's mixed housing means results vary widely from one listing to the next. A newer slab home and a century-old town center home are different radon situations and can produce very different inspection outcomes.

Sellers: testing before you list moves the whole conversation onto your schedule instead of into the inspection window. Buyers: order the radon test early in the inspection period, because a test needs 48 hours minimum under closed-house conditions and ordering it on the last day is how contingency deadlines get extended.
