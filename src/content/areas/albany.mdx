## Rural Delaware County is a different radon job

Albany sits in the northeast corner of Delaware County, and the housing out this way looks different from Muncie's. More crawl spaces. More original farmhouse construction with additions. More private wells. Fewer of the full poured basements that most radon content on the internet quietly assumes you have.

None of that changes the underlying risk. Delaware County is EPA Zone 1 throughout, and the soil that produces radon in Muncie is the same soil out here. What changes is the mitigation approach, and in the case of crawl spaces, the price.

## Crawl spaces: the main event out here

If your home is on a crawl space, most of what you have read about radon systems does not apply to you, because it describes coring a slab you do not have.

A crawl space with a dirt floor, or with the loose builder's vapor barrier that got rolled out decades ago and sealed at nothing, is an open pathway across its entire area. And the crawl space is not isolated from the house the way people assume: air moves up through floor penetrations, ductwork chases, and plumbing gaps, so a meaningful share of the air on your first floor came up out of there.

The fix is **sub-membrane depressurization**: radon-grade reinforced liner sealed across the floor, around every pier, and up the foundation walls, with a suction point drawn from beneath it and vented above the roofline. The physics is identical to a sub-slab system. The barrier just gets installed rather than poured.

A useful side effect worth counting: a properly sealed crawl space is usually a drier crawl space, which is good for joists, subfloor, and the musty smell that migrates upstairs. What it is not is waterproofing. If water actively enters the space, that is a drainage problem to solve first. Full detail on the [crawl space radon page](/services/crawl-space-radon/).

<Callout tone="warn" title="Encapsulation is not mitigation">
  <p class="mb-0">If a contractor has quoted you crawl space encapsulation and described it as handling your radon, ask three questions: is there a fan, does it discharge above the roofline, and is a post-mitigation test included? If any answer is no, you are buying a moisture project. It will probably reduce radon somewhat, because it blocks the easiest path, but "somewhat" and "below the action level" are different outcomes and only a test tells you which one you got.</p>

## Radon in private well water

This is the piece that rarely gets covered, and it matters disproportionately out here because so many rural Delaware County homes draw from private wells.

Groundwater passing through uranium-bearing rock picks up dissolved radon. That radon stays in solution until the water is agitated, at which point it comes out into your indoor air. Showering is the big one; dishwashers and washing machines contribute.

Municipal supplies are treated and monitored, and systems drawing from surface water are generally not a concern. **Private wells are neither treated nor monitored**, which is the entire distinction.

The practical order of operations:

1. **Test your indoor air first.** That is where the health risk is measured, and for the large majority of homes, including most with some waterborne radon, soil gas is the dominant source.
2. **If the air result is high, mitigate the soil gas.** That is where the return is.
3. **Retest after mitigation.** This is the step that tells you what you are dealing with.
4. **If the retest did not come down as far as the design predicted, test the water.** A soil-gas system that is working correctly but leaves you higher than expected is the classic signature of a second source.

Waterborne radon is treated at the point of entry, typically with aeration or granular activated carbon, and it is a separate system from the one on your foundation. Air testing is covered on the [radon testing page](/services/radon-testing/).

## Older foundations and combination systems

Farmhouse-era construction around Albany, Eaton, and Gaston tends to have accumulated additions: an original structure on fieldstone or block, a mid-century addition on a crawl space, a later one on a slab. Each section can have its own foundation type, and they are frequently not connected below grade.

That means one suction point in one section may do nothing for the rest of the house, and the only way to know is to measure. A pressure field diagnostic before the quote is what separates a system that works everywhere from a system that works in the original farmhouse and nowhere else. The [radon mitigation page](/services/radon-mitigation/) covers combination systems in more detail.
