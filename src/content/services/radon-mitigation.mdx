## When you actually need a radon mitigation system

If a test came back at **4.0 pCi/L or higher**, mitigate. That is the EPA action level, it is not a soft recommendation, and in Delaware County it is a threshold the average home is already sitting on.

Between **2.0 and 3.9 pCi/L**, EPA says to consider it, and this is where the honest conversation happens. There is no established threshold below which radon is harmless. The risk curve doesn't have a cliff, it has a slope. But the slope at 3.3 is genuinely flatter than at 8.0, and pretending otherwise to sell a system is exactly the behavior that makes people distrust this industry.

Three things push a mid-range reading toward "fix it":

- **Somebody lives down there.** A finished basement bedroom, home office, or rec room turns an abstract average into hours of daily exposure at the highest-concentration point in the house.
- **You tested in warm weather.** Muncie closed-house winter readings routinely come in above the same home's summer number. A 3.3 in July is not the same as a 3.3 in January.
- **You're selling within a couple of years.** Mitigating on your schedule costs the same as mitigating under a two-week closing deadline, minus the leverage the buyer would otherwise have.

Below **2.0 pCi/L**, retest every two years and move on with your life.

  <p class="mb-0">Radon is colorless, odorless, and tasteless at every concentration. There is no smell in the basement, no headache, no stain on the wall, no symptom that shows up before the disease does. A house at 18 pCi/L feels exactly like a house at 0.4. The only way to know is to <a href="/services/radon-testing/">measure it</a>, which is also why the "my house feels fine" instinct is the single most expensive assumption in radon.</p>

## System types, matched to Delaware County foundations

Almost every residential radon system is a variation on one idea: depressurize the soil under the house so gas flows into a pipe instead of into your living room. Which variation you need is decided by what your house sits on.

### Sub-slab depressurization (basements and slab-on-grade)

The standard system, and what most Muncie homes get. A core hole goes through the slab, a suction pit is excavated in the aggregate below it, and sealed PVC carries the gas from that pit to a fan mounted outside conditioned space: in the garage, on an exterior wall, or in the attic. The pipe terminates above the roofline and away from windows.

The engineering that actually matters is invisible from the driveway: whether the sub-slab aggregate lets the suction field spread across the whole footprint, or whether tight clay soil means you need a second suction point. A pressure-field extension diagnostic answers that before anyone commits to a price.

### Sub-membrane depressurization (crawl spaces)

A crawl space has no slab to depressurize, so the floor gets one: heavy-gauge reinforced liner sealed to the foundation walls and around every pier, with a suction point drawn from underneath it. It costs more than a slab system because most of the labor is hand-work in a three-foot space. A large share of Delaware County housing sits on crawl spaces, so this is not the exotic case here. Details on the [crawl space radon page](/services/crawl-space-radon/).

### Combination and multi-point systems

Additions, split-levels, and houses where a slab section meets a crawl space usually need both approaches tied into one fan, or two suction points manifolded together. Older Muncie housing stock produces this more often than newer subdivisions do.

## What it costs in Muncie, and what moves the number

Most single-family systems here land between **$1,200 and $2,000 installed**. Indiana's state fact sheet cites an $800 to $1,200 average, which describes the easiest possible job: accessible basement, one suction point, short pipe run, nothing in the way.

Within that range, a straightforward basement or slab job with one suction point sits at the low end. Crawl space systems run higher than slab systems because of the membrane labor. Replacing a fan on an existing system costs a fraction of a new install, since the pipe and the routing are already there.

We are not going to publish a precise dollar figure for your specific house on a web page, because the number genuinely depends on things nobody can see from the street. What we will do is tell you the range before anyone visits.

The variables that move you within that range:

- **Foundation type.** Crawl spaces cost more because of the membrane labor, not because the physics is harder.
- **Footprint and soil.** A 2,600 sq ft slab over tight clay may need a second suction point that a 1,200 sq ft slab over gravel never will.
- **Pipe routing.** An interior route through a garage and out the roof is cheaper and better-looking than a long exterior run up two stories of siding.
- **Fan sizing.** Static pressure requirements drive fan selection, and the right fan is the one that matches your soil, not the biggest one on the truck.

## Timeline and what living with a system is like

Most Muncie installs are **one day**, typically four to six hours. Crawl space membrane jobs and multi-suction systems can run into a second day; you will know which one you are before work starts, not after.

Once it is in, the system runs continuously and you will mostly forget it exists. A correctly installed fan mounted outside is a low hum you notice from the driveway and not at all from inside. It adds roughly $4–$10 a month to the electric bill. Maintenance is: glance at the manometer once a season, and replace the fan at around year ten.

## The retest is the part that matters

An installed system that nobody verified is a decoration. Every job should end with a **post-mitigation test**, and the target is a result under the 4.0 pCi/L action level. A correctly designed system should bring a home well below that, and the test is what tells you whether yours did.

If a post-mitigation test does not come down where it should, the fix is not a new system, it is diagnosing why. Usually it's an unsealed pathway, an undersized fan, or a slab section outside the suction field. That diagnosis is the installer's job to complete, and it is the standard you should hold any radon contractor to, including anyone we route you to.

Keep that retest document. When you sell, it is the difference between "there's a system" and "there's a system and here is proof it works."

## Building new? Make it radon-ready instead

If you are building in Delaware County, passive radon-ready rough-in during construction costs a few hundred dollars: a gas-permeable aggregate layer, a sealed vapor barrier, a capped vertical pipe from below the slab up through the roof, and a junction box in the attic. If the finished house tests high, activating it means adding a fan to a pipe that already exists, which costs a fraction of a retrofit.

In a Zone 1 county, this is the cheapest insurance in the entire build. Ask your builder before the slab is poured, because afterward the conversation becomes a retrofit conversation.
