## Who this page is for

- **Schools and childcare facilities**, where the occupants are children spending long hours in ground-floor rooms
- **Apartment buildings, condos, and other multifamily property**, where every ground-contact unit is its own exposure question and the owner carries the liability
- **Offices, medical and dental practices, and workplaces** with occupied ground-floor or below-grade space
- **Churches, community buildings, and nonprofits** with finished basements in regular use
- **Buyers and lenders in commercial real estate transactions**, where radon has become a standard environmental due-diligence line item

## Why building size changes the problem

A single-family house is one pressure envelope over one foundation. A commercial building usually is not.

**The footprint is larger, and soil is not uniform underneath it.** One corner of a building can read very differently from another. This is why a large building gets a grid of measurements rather than a sample.

**Mechanical ventilation dominates the pressure relationships.** A residential radon system fights the modest stack effect of a house. A commercial building has air handlers, make-up air, and exhaust systems that can put whole zones under negative pressure relative to the soil, actively drawing soil gas in. Sometimes the most effective first move is an HVAC balancing change rather than a mitigation system.

**Occupancy constrains everything.** You cannot vacate a school for a week or shut down a leasing office. Work gets phased, scheduled after hours, and sequenced so occupied space stays usable.

**The deliverable is documentation.** For a house, the outcome is a lower number. For a commercial building, the outcome is a lower number plus a report that a school board, a lender, an insurer, a licensing agency, or a buyer's environmental consultant will read and rely on.

## Testing protocols

**Measurement placement.** Every ground-contact occupied room, which in a school means classrooms rather than a sample of the hallway, and in multifamily means every ground-contact unit. Frequently occupied below-grade spaces are included regardless of their nominal use.

**Method.** Continuous radon monitors are the practical default for commercial work. Hourly data lets you see the influence of HVAC cycling and weather, and it produces a defensible record. Charcoal detectors have a role in high-count deployments where cost per point matters.

**Conditions.** Testing is performed under normal building operating conditions, with HVAC running as it does day to day. A test conducted with the air handlers off does not describe the building anyone actually occupies.

**Timing.** Winter, if the schedule allows, for the same closed-building reasons that make winter the conservative season in residential testing.

**Follow-up.** Any room over the action level gets a confirmation measurement before mitigation is designed, because mitigation designed off a single anomalous reading is expensive to get wrong.

## Mitigation design for larger buildings

The underlying method is still active soil depressurization. What changes is that it becomes an engineering exercise rather than a standard install.

Design typically starts with **diagnostics**: pressure field extension testing at candidate locations to find out how far suction actually travels under this specific slab, given this specific sub-slab material. That measurement determines how many suction points the building needs and where they go, and no honest fixed price exists before it.

From there: **multiple suction points** manifolded into properly sized fans, often with more than one fan system across a large footprint; **HVAC integration**, since pressure relationships have to be accounted for and sometimes adjusted; **routing** that respects the building's structure, finishes, and occupancy; and **monitoring**, because on a commercial system it is worth having something better than a manometer somebody has to remember to look at.

Then post-mitigation testing across the same measurement grid, which is the document that closes the project.

## What you get in writing

For a commercial client the paperwork is a real part of the deliverable:

- Full measurement report: device placement, serials and calibration, start and stop times, building operating conditions, and results per location
- System design documentation, including diagnostic findings and the basis for the suction point layout
- Post-mitigation verification across the measurement grid
- As-built documentation of what was installed and where
- A monitoring and maintenance schedule, since fans on commercial systems age the same way residential fans do

That package is what satisfies a board, a lender's environmental review, an insurer, or a licensing agency, and it is what a future buyer's consultant will ask for.
