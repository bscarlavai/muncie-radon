## The problem with a system nobody checks

There are a lot of radon mitigation systems in Delaware County. In a Zone 1 county with an average indoor level at the action level, they have been going in on real estate transactions for years, and every one of them has a fan on it with a finite life.

Here is the failure mode that makes this worth a page of its own: **a dead radon fan produces no symptom.** No alarm, no light, no smell, no change you would notice. The white pipe is still on the side of the house. From the outside, a system that stopped working three years ago and a system running perfectly are indistinguishable.

Meanwhile the house returns to its pre-mitigation level over a period of days to weeks. A home that tested at 9.0, got a system, retested at 1.4, and lost its fan in year eleven is a 9.0 house again, and the family living in it has every reason to believe they solved this problem a decade ago.

<Callout tone="warn" title="Check the manometer. It takes five seconds.">
  <p class="mb-0">The manometer is the small U-shaped clear tube mounted on your radon pipe, usually near where it enters the wall, with colored liquid in it. <strong>If the liquid sits at different heights in the two columns, the system is pulling suction and running.</strong> If both columns are level with each other, the fan is not moving air. That is the entire test. Do it seasonally, and take a photo of the reading when the system is known-good so you have a baseline to compare against.</p>
</Callout>

## Signs your system has stopped working

- **Level manometer.** The definitive sign. Both columns of liquid at the same height means no suction.
- **Silence.** If you could always hear a faint hum near the fan and now you cannot, the motor is not turning.
- **New noises.** Grinding, rattling, or a rising and falling pitch means bearings are wearing. This is your warning shot before failure.
- **A retest that came back high.** If a house with a system tests above the action level, something in the system is not doing its job.
- **The system is a decade old.** Age alone is reason enough to check.
- **Visible damage.** Cracked pipe, a disconnected joint, a fan housing damaged by weather or a ladder, or a discharge point that got blocked.

A note on the last one: the manometer measures suction in the pipe, so it tells you the fan is moving air. It does not tell you the air is going where it should, or that a seal in the crawl space has not failed. That is what a retest is for.

## What fan replacement involves

Straightforward work, assuming the rest of the system is sound. The old fan comes off, the new fan goes on in the same location using the existing pipe and couplings, the electrical connection is remade, and the system restarts. Most replacements are a short visit.

The judgment call is fan selection, and it is worth insisting on. The replacement should be sized to the static pressure your specific system actually needs, which is not automatically the same model that was originally installed. If the original system was underperforming, replacing like-for-like reproduces the original underperformance.

Cost is a fraction of a full system, since the pipe, routing, sealing, and electrical are already in place. It is quoted after the system is looked at, because the answer depends on what fan the system needs and whether anything else has degraded in ten years of Indiana weather.

## Fixing the systems that never worked right

The second half of this page is for a specific and fairly common situation: a system that is running exactly as designed and still not achieving the number it should. Usually a flip house, a DIY install, or the cheapest bid on a transaction where somebody just needed a pipe on the house before closing.

What tends to be wrong:

- **Undersized fan.** The most common. A low-suction fan on tight clay soil moves air through the pipe, satisfies the manometer, and never develops a suction field across the whole slab.
- **Too few suction points.** A large or subdivided slab needs more than one. A single point at one corner of a 2,600 square foot footprint leaves most of the house outside the field.
- **Unsealed sump pit.** A wide-open pit lets the fan pull easy air instead of soil gas, which short-circuits the whole system. One of the cheapest fixes with one of the largest effects.
- **Unsealed slab penetrations and joints.** Same principle, distributed across more openings.
- **Improper discharge.** Terminating below the roofline, too near a window, or into a soffit lets discharged gas re-enter the house.
- **Interior fan placement.** A fan mounted in conditioned space puts the pressurized side of the system inside the house, so any leak downstream of the fan pushes radon into your living area rather than out of it.

The diagnostic sequence is the same regardless: verify the fan is running, verify suction, then test the house. If the fan runs, the manometer shows suction, and the number is still high, the problem is in the design or the sealing, not the fan.

## Retesting after any repair

Every repair should end with a test. Replacing a fan restores suction; only a measurement tells you it restored the level.

Use a short-term test, at least 48 hours under closed-house conditions, and keep the result with your home records alongside the original post-mitigation test. Over the life of a house, that small stack of paper is what proves the system has been maintained, and it is what the next buyer's agent will ask for.

Details on test types and what makes a result defensible are on the [radon testing page](/services/radon-testing/).
